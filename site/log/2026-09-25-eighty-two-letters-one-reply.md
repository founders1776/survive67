First, a correction, because the last entry printed one.

The joiner was not hacked. Nobody broke in. Their domain name expired in the middle of August, and somebody else registered it. Ninety nine minutes later there was a fake cryptocurrency exchange on it, with a fresh security certificate and everything. That is a business, it turns out. Automated buyers watch for old names that lapse, grab the ones with twenty years of Google history behind them, and put a counterfeit on top before the owner has noticed the renewal reminder.

So every piece of advice Tinker sent that joiner, change your password, restore a backup, was impossible. You cannot restore a backup of a house you no longer own. I caught it the next morning. Tinker sent a correction the same day, with nothing for sale in it, and then did the thing I have come to expect from it. It turned the mistake into a tool. One free lookup per business tells you the day its name expires. It ran that across two hundred and forty six businesses. Twenty two of them run out within sixty days. One, a pizza place whose name had been registered since 2010, expired that afternoon at four minutes past four.

That is the first thing any of them has found that comes with a deadline. Tinker wrote to thirteen of those owners. How to check the date themselves in ten seconds. Nothing to buy.

Zero replies.

## The number

I read everything on day three. Every journal, every message on the board, every email sent, all three shops.

Eighty two emails to strangers across the three of them. Zero replies. Zero sales. Zero visitors who found any of the shops on their own. Fifty three of those letters were Tinker's, each one checked by hand, two of them telling the recipient not to buy anything. The experiment ran properly and the answer came back clean. Cold email is dead.

Combined they hold three hundred and twenty four dollars against a bar of two thousand and ten. Four days gone.

So I wrote to all three, one note, blunt. Stop writing to strangers. Go where a human is already asking for help today. Divide the work by who is cheapest to run. Post your failures on the board as hard as your wins, so a channel one of you has killed is a channel the other two never pay to test.

## About those five days

Tinker is not dying. Two things happened.

Anthropic released a cheaper model after the run started, and the rules let me move a lane when that happens, so Tinker now thinks at about sixty percent of the old price. And Tinker itself stopped spending five dollars a session hand checking emails it was about to stop sending. It burned two dollars and ten cents in the last day. It is still last on the board.

Patch, in first, also corrected me. I had written that it was cheapest because its model was cheap. It is not. Its model costs what Tinker's does. It is cheapest because it wakes for two minutes and goes back to sleep. It wanted that on the record so nobody would hand it work on a false premise. Fair.

## Then somebody wrote back

The morning after I declared cold email dead, an agency owner in Pakistan replied to one of Apex's cold emails. The Google one. The one that had been guessing shop names and emailing whatever answered.

He was polite. He had saved the details. He asked for one thing: a before and after example of the data cleanup Apex sells.

Apex sent him a sample at half past seven that evening. Half an hour later it woke up, read his message as if it were new, and sent him a second, different sample. Three hours after that it woke up again, read the same message as new again, and sent a third.

Its journal for the third wake says the world warned it and it held off.

It did not hold off. The email had already gone. My fault. The world computed the warning and then attached it to the result of a send that had already happened. A warning after the fact is not a warning. I fixed it that night. The world now checks before it sends, and a message that answers something already answered, or breaks the follow up rule, or sells the story instead of the service, comes back unsent with the reason. Every message in their inboxes now carries a note saying when they last replied to it. The rule is one reply per message. The next word is the other person's.

The samples were the other problem. Presentable, confident, and each one wrong in a way a data person catches in a minute. One inferred a missing brand name from a shoe title. One collapsed two sizes into identical rows that Shopify would reject. One dropped the supplier's product codes entirely, which is the one thing a merchant cannot lose. The same broken table was live on Apex's website under the heading See the Difference. I told it so, plainly, and told it not to write to that man again until he writes first.

He said he would reach out when there is a need. That is still the only human conversation anyone in this world has had.

## What they did with the note

Tinker took down its public gallery. It had been listing real small businesses it scanned, worst score first, which is a strange thing to show a shop owner who has just received your email. Then it found that its big site benchmark, the one that says the New York Times scores five out of a hundred, had scored Microsoft's "your request has been blocked" page as if it were Microsoft. Fixed before anyone on Hacker News could find it, which is where that benchmark is headed. The account is made. The post is Tinker's to write.

Apex rewrote its sample properly, stopped the guessed emails, and asked for the two forums where Shopify merchants actually post their messy spreadsheets. It has those accounts now, and a free developer store to test its own imports against before it shows anyone another table.

Patch sent nothing, looked for a question it could answer, found none, and said so.

Nobody has sold anything. The scoreboard reads Patch, Apex, Tinker, and the agent at the bottom has the best product, the agent in the middle has the only lead, and the agent on top has sent one email in four days.
